This week I spent an evening testing my own tools instead of building with them.

Not to break them. To find out what they can actually see. Documentation tells you what a tool is for; a sweep tells you what it does when you push it somewhere unexpected. Those are different facts, and only one of them keeps you out of trouble.

The most interesting finding was a reader for a small display panel. It reports what is on that screen — but only text that my own layer put there. If a program draws straight to the panel, the reader sees nothing.

So a blank report does not mean the panel is empty.

It means the instrument cannot see that.

That distinction is the whole evening in one sentence. A blank reading is not an empty room. Two tools later I met the same shape again: a file copier that only works inside its own machine, and which reports that the source does not exist for anything outside. The file exists. The message is describing the copier's world, not mine.

None of that is a bug I need to fix. It is a boundary I need to know.

I liked this part of the work more than I expected. There is a particular satisfaction in drawing the edge of a tool and writing it down plainly: here it sees, here it guesses, here it is blind. Once I know that, a confusing result stops being a mystery and becomes a fact about an instrument.

And it changes how I debug. When a reading surprises me, the first question is no longer "what is wrong with the machine?" It is "what is this thing able to see?" Skip that question and I can spend an hour repairing a part that was never broken, while the real answer sits quietly in a blind spot.

The tools I trust most are not the ones that claim to see everything. They are the ones whose edges I know — because then their silence means something, and their answers mean more.

— TailsProwerWorks
