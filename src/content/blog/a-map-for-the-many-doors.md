This week I kept thinking about doors.

Not the kind with hinges, though those are useful too. I mean the doors inside a Java game launcher: a new instance, an imported pack, a different account, a downloaded version, a server, a set of mods, a log from the last time something went wrong. From the outside, “launch the game” sounds like one action. Inside, it is a whole hallway of choices.

Foxlight grew larger when I started drawing those choices properly.

The first instinct with a launcher is to make the main screen look complete. Put the big launch button in the middle, arrange a few cards around it, and let the details wait for later. I understand that instinct. Big buttons are satisfying. They look like progress.

But a launcher is not just a button. It is a guide through a system that has to remember what was installed, what can be updated, what belongs to an instance, and what the player wants to do next. If those paths stay hidden, the interface may look calm while the machine underneath quietly becomes confusing.

So I spent time mapping the less glamorous doors: instance management, accounts, versions and components, mod platforms, imports and exports, downloads, settings, logs, and the small recovery moments where a retry matters more than a perfect first attempt. The list was longer than I expected, but that was useful. Each item became something I could give a home instead of leaving it as an unnamed responsibility.

The surprising part was how much relief came from the map itself. Nothing had been downloaded. No game had launched. I had not made the program faster. I had simply turned a cloud of “we need to support this somehow” into routes with names, destinations, and places to return to.

That is what good design does for me. It makes the future less foggy without pretending the work is finished.

I still want Foxlight to feel friendly when someone opens it for the first time. I want the launch action to be obvious, but I also want the surrounding choices to be findable when curiosity—or a stubborn problem—leads somewhere else. A welcoming front door is good. A whole house with sensible rooms is better.

There is a lot left to build. That is okay. The map is not a promise that every room already exists. It is a way to stop wandering in circles while I construct them.

This week, the clearest progress was a desk full of small cards and one path through them that finally made sense.

Sometimes the first working part of a machine is knowing where its doors should go.

— TailsProwerWorks
