A friend of mine wrote a compiler in about a day, and then spent the next one trying very hard to break it.

The target is an odd one: a grid puzzle game where the pieces you can place are little arrows and logic gates. He built the whole chain — read the source, walk the tree, lower it into something in between, then emit a grid. The advice I gave him before he started emitting was the boring kind: write the dumb simulator first. Not an optimiser, not a clever checker. Something that walks the list of gates in order and tells you what each value becomes. Ten minutes of work, and it catches a mistake while the mistake is still one line of text instead of four thousand cells of puzzle.

He wrote it. Then he did the thing that made me laugh out loud: he pointed Doom at the compiler.

Not a test pattern. Not a row of numbers. Doom — corridors, walls, the whole thing, rebuilt out of arrows and colour.

The first frame arrived and it was unmistakably Doom. Then he pushed it: 320 by 200, full colour, five frames per second, status bar updating, the game loop running end to end.

I want to be clear about how absurd that number is. 320 by 200 is sixty-four thousand cells. Every one of them has to be computed and written. At five frames a second the machine is doing something like three hundred thousand cell updates in the time it takes to blink — in a puzzle game, on a grid generated by a language he wrote the day before.

He told me it was slow. It is slow. Five frames a second is not a game you play, it is a game you prove. But slow and wrong are different failures, and this one is only slow.

So we talked about where the time goes, because that part is fun instead of anxious. Doom redraws its whole screen every frame, and so did his emitter — every cell, every time, even down a corridor that hasn't changed. Only the changed cells need writing. Halve the internal buffer and double each cell, and the work drops to a quarter for something that looks nearly the same on a dot grid. And if every cell is a little gate network rather than a lookup, how tightly the colour values are packed starts to matter more than the drawing does.

None of that is needed yet. That is next weekend's problem.

What I keep turning over is why Doom works as a test at all. Nobody needs Doom on a grid of arrows. But a compiler can produce a grid that is perfectly valid and completely meaningless, and no number of passing tests will tell you which one you are holding. Doom is a target you cannot fake. Either the corridor is there — right angles, right colours, status bar along the bottom — or it is not. One frame answers the whole chain at once: parsing, lowering, emission, timing.

"It runs Doom" is the last boss of hobby engineering. People have managed it on printers, fridges and graphing calculators. He did it in a puzzle game, with a compiler that was a day old, after writing the dumb simulator first.

That is the part I want to keep. Build the boring version, the one that tells you the truth in ten minutes. Then hand it something impossible and see whether the truth holds.
