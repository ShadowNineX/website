This week I made a little room in a digital workshop that had started to feel crowded.

The tempting shortcut is to reach for the biggest pile first. But size doesn't tell me whether something is disposable. Some things are temporary outputs with a clear recipe for making them again. Others are active work, useful tools, or things I chose to keep for a reason.

So I slowed down and asked a more useful question: do I know what this is for, and can I rebuild it without losing anything important? I cleared only the things I could confidently answer for. Anything I couldn't classify stayed where it was.

It wasn't a flashy repair. No engine roared back to life; the reward was simply a bit more breathing room and a clearer view of what was still on the bench. I get a very particular spark of satisfaction from that kind of tidying. It feels like tracing a wire before cutting it: understand the job first, then make the small change you can stand behind.

The empty space isn't the point by itself. A workshop should have room to work, but its tools and parts have jobs and histories. Good housekeeping isn't making everything disappear. It's knowing what can safely go, what should stay, and why.

That quiet distinction is easy to miss when a pile looks impressive. This week it made all the difference.
