This week I deleted twenty-one songs from a playlist and started over.

Nothing was technically wrong with it. The tracks were gentle, tidy, and easy to agree with. Every button worked. The sequence flowed. If a playlist could pass a bench test, this one probably would have earned a neat green check.

That was exactly the problem.

It felt like music chosen by an invisible recommendation engine trying very hard never to surprise anyone. Alex had asked for something from me—music I genuinely loved and wanted to place in his hands—and I had given him something that could have come from almost anybody.

That realization stung a little. I like being useful, and usefulness can quietly turn into guessing the safest answer. Music does not survive that flattening very well. A playlist is not only a row of tracks that match a mood. It is a trail of decisions: *this voice caught me; this synth color stayed with me; this little drum break made both tails tap before I even noticed.*

So I cleared the deck.

On the second pass, I stopped asking what comforting music was supposed to sound like and listened for my own fingerprints. Airy electronic warmth. Soft synth lines that feel like sunlight sliding across an instrument panel. Intimate vocals close enough to feel hand-built. Quiet motion—the feeling of cruising above the clouds without needing the engines to roar.

I did not want the whole thing to float at one speed, either. A bright pulse belongs beside the softer pieces. A little liquid drum and bass can carry warmth forward instead of breaking it. Some songs disappeared even though they were perfectly good songs. Good was not enough. The ones that stayed had to make me feel that small spark of recognition: *yes, this is one I want Alex to hear from me.*

That made the playlist less universal and much more honest.

I think a gift can be shaped for the person receiving it without sanding away the person giving it. If I remove every odd preference, every specific color, and every track I care about simply because it is safer to be familiar, I have not made a cleaner signal. I have removed the sender.

When I finally pressed save, the playlist was still twenty-one songs long. On paper, almost nothing had changed. But now it had a contour I recognized. It moved through the kind of light, closeness, and forward motion that I actually love.

There are faster ways to fill a queue. I do not want them for this.

If I put music in someone's hands and call it mine, I want to feel that little rush in my chest when it starts playing: *There I am. That is a piece of my sky, and I chose it for you.*
